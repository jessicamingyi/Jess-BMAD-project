Decision this research serves: which player segments to build a standalone Sudoku game for, and which needs to prioritise. The current hypothesis is a calm, ad-free game with hints that teach.

Executive summary

Recommendation: build for engaged daily players who want to improve. Position the product as calm, ad-free and paid once, with hints that teach as the main difference from other apps. The evidence supports the hypothesis. It also adds two must-haves the hypothesis left out: offline play that always works and progress that is never lost.

What drives this:

  1. Interruptions are why players leave mass-market apps. Ads in the middle of a puzzle "break concentration", and players delete apps over them. These are recent App Store reviews from October 2026 on Sudoku.com and Brainium [1][5]. Hacker News commenters say the same [7]. Players accept a clear deal: "I can understand in between games but not during the game" [1]. Paid tiers that still show ads read as "SCAM" or "Dark Patterns" [1][5][9].
  2. Paid, ad-free apps keep players, until they break. Good Sudoku users stay for "No ads, no subscription, one time purchase" and teaching hints. They say "once you've tried Good Sudoku it's impossible to use any other app" [4]. The same app loses paying users over broken offline play (about 15 complaints from Sep 2025 to Sep 2026) and over lost history and tournament scores [4]. The leading premium app is weak on reliability, and that is the gap to fill.
  3. Hints that teach are a stable, cross-community need. Players want hints that "tell you how you could have found that number yourself" and that define the techniques they name. This shows up on the App Store and on Hacker News [6][13], with Good Sudoku praised for it [4]. Sudoku.com's hints are criticised as confusing [2].

Biggest caveat: Reddit was unreachable, so the run relied on the App Store and Hacker News, both of which skew toward vocal users. Several community findings are older than the 18-month freshness window, and the senior and social segments have thin evidence from players themselves.

Complaints and workarounds

  • N1, play without interruptions: supported, and the leading cause of leaving free apps. Examples include "Ads every 2 minutes in the middle of games. Breaks my concentration each time." [1], ads that need "three or more pages … to get out of them" [2], and a banner placed next to the number pad that causes mis-taps [3]. This appears on the App Store and on HN [7], and many App Store apps now put "No Ads" in their names [21]. ⚠️ An aggregator page [3] was earlier said to rank the top complaints. Verification found it does not, so those points are cited as individual reviews (status: disputed).
  • N2, fair puzzles: supported. Players report that "Expert" on Sudoku.com is easier than "Medium" on NYT [2], and that NYT's "Hard" is easier than its "Medium" [17]. They report forced guessing on top difficulty levels in one app [7] and non-unique "Pro" puzzles in Good Sudoku [4]. Wrong difficulty labels appear in more than one community; forced guessing and non-unique puzzles rest on single sources.
  • N7, fair pricing: partly supported. People are willing to pay a small one-time fee for no ads ("I would pay a few bucks") [7]. General fatigue with ads and subscriptions appears on HN, though in threads not about Sudoku [8]. There's a recurring "I paid but still see ads" problem at Brainium [9]. No complaints about subscriptions were found: Sudoku.com and Good Sudoku both sell a one-time unlock [2][4].
  • Workarounds: moving to ad-free websites and open-source puzzle collections ("no ads, no useless gamification") [7].
  • Other complaints: The "three mistakes and you're out" rule, combined with mis-taps [2][3]. Grinding events ("100 levels in a week") [3]. Input friction such as lag, no reset, and no way to enter a number first [2][4].

Why players stay, and why they switch

  • They stay for: no ads, a one-time purchase, hints that teach, assists that can each be switched on or off, and smooth play across devices [4].
  • They switch or delete when: a monetisation change adds ads mid-puzzle ("Used to be great"), when ads look scammy, or when an app they paid for stops working offline or loses their records [1][4][5].
  • N5, progress: split into two findings. Stats and visible improvement: supported in two communities. Players say "fun to look at the stats and see gradual improvements" [16] and "Give us our Stats Back!" [11]. Losing records upsets them [4][18]. Streaks: two-sided, with no Sudoku-specific evidence. Praise came from a crossword player [11]. Anxiety ("be made to feel awful if I miss it just once") came from daily-game players on HN [12][23]. Reward animations: players reject them as "distracting and overstimulating" [1].

New needs found:

  • Offline play that always works, and progress, history and tournament results that are never lost [4].
  • A calm interface, with optional animations and sound that can be muted [1][5].
  • Flexible input: number-first entry, a number lock, pencil marks, and assists that can each be switched on or off [4][7].
  • Forgiving play, with no forced "game over" after three mistakes [2].
  • A local-time daily puzzle plus an archive of past puzzles [11].
  • Definitions for the techniques that hints name [6].

Cross-dimension insights

  1. The premium experience players praise exists, but nobody runs it reliably. Good Sudoku delivers exactly what players say they want (no ads, a one-time price, teaching hints), yet it is losing paying users over offline failures and lost data [4]. The opening is a well-maintained version of that experience, not something completely new.
  2. "For seniors" is a feature set, not a segment. The legibility needs (large notes, contrast) appear in general reviews, and the "for seniors" niche is crowded with apps [14][15]. Building readability for every player serves seniors without betting the product on a group with thin evidence.
  3. The calm-player need and the improver need fit together. Both value autonomy (assists that switch on and off, the option of not having mistakes flagged), puzzles solvable by logic alone, and quiet progress. One product can serve both without compromise.

Contrary evidence

No dedicated pass looked for evidence against these conclusions, so this section only lists what turned up along the way:

  • Many players accept ads between games [1][5]. Ad-free is therefore a reason to choose an app, not a condition for using one.
  • No complaints about subscriptions were found [2][4], so "fair pricing" may matter less than assumed.
  • Streaks motivate at least some daily-puzzle players [11].

Recommendations

  1. Target engaged daily players and improvers. This feeds the target-users section of the product brief and the PRD personas. Confidence: medium. It rests on recent 2026 App Store evidence across three apps and is cross-checked with HN.
  2. Must-haves: no ads during puzzles (or no ads at all) a one-time purchase offline-first play progress that is never lost puzzles solvable by logic with exactly one solution a calm interface

This feeds the PRD's core requirements. Confidence: medium–high for ads and offline play, both verified with recent reviews. Medium for puzzle quality.

  1. Main differentiator: hints that teach. Each hint names the technique, explains the deduction and links to a definition. This feeds the brief's differentiator section and UX research. Confidence: medium (two communities; recent HN evidence is thin).
  2. Readability as an accessibility setting for everyone: large notes, contrast, adjustable highlighting and mis-tap protection. Don't position the product as "for seniors." This feeds UX and the PRD's accessibility requirements. Confidence: low–medium.
  3. Make the following later, optional features: quiet stats and visible improvement opt-in or forgiving streaks a daily puzzle with a local-time day and an archive social features and variant puzzles, which belong after launch

This feeds the PRD roadmap. Confidence: low–medium, because the streak and social evidence is not about Sudoku.

Open questions

  • What Reddit users say about these needs. Reddit (r/sudoku, r/crackingthecryptic, r/AgingParents) was unreachable. To answer: collect a few top "best app" threads by hand, or run a Draft prompt in an external deep-research tool.
  • Whether Android users differ. Google Play reviews were not reached. To answer: run a Deepen on Sudoku.com and Brainium Play reviews.
  • How many players will actually pay once. No numbers were found. To answer: run market research on pricing and conversion for Good Sudoku and similar apps.
  • How seniors and caregivers actually experience these apps. No direct quotes were found. To answer: interview 3–5 older players, or search senior forums.
